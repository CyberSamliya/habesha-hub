Government aid & benefitsየመንግስት እርዳታ እና ጥቅማ ጥቅሞች

What each program actually covers, who it's usually for, and where to apply. Always apply through the official portal linked below — never pay anyone to "guarantee" approval.

Food

SNAP (food assistance)

Monthly funds for groceries, loaded onto an EBT card. Many lawfully present immigrants and refugees qualify; rules on waiting periods vary by status.

Check eligibility (USDA) →
Health

Medicaid & CHIP

Free or low-cost health coverage based on income and household size. Refugees and asylees are generally eligible on arrival; other statuses may have a waiting period.

Find your state Medicaid office →
Cash

TANF (cash assistance)

Temporary monthly cash support for families with children, plus job-readiness services. Applied for through your state's family services office.

Program overview (HHS) →
Housing

Housing assistance

Section 8 vouchers and public housing lower your rent based on income. Waitlists are long in most cities — apply as early as possible.

Section 8 basics (HUD) →
Refugee

Refugee Cash & Resettlement

Extra federal support in the first months after arrival — cash, medical, and employment help, on top of any state programs.

Office of Refugee Resettlement →
Income

Unemployment & SSI/SSDI

Unemployment covers job loss through your state workforce agency. SSI/SSDI support people who can't work due to a disability, with separate rules for non-citizens.

Immigration & legal guidanceየስደት እና ህጋዊ መመሪያ

This section explains terms in plain language and points you to who can actually help. It is not legal advice, and immigration consequences depend heavily on individual facts.

Who should I talk to first?
Free and low-cost legal help
+

For anything involving your immigration status — a filing deadline, a status change, a notice from USCIS or immigration court — talk to a licensed immigration attorney or an accredited nonprofit legal service before you act. A mistake on a form or a missed deadline can have consequences that are hard to undo.

  • USCIS.gov — official forms, filing fees, and case status, direct from the government.
  • Immigrant Legal Resource Center — plain-language guides and a directory of legal help.
  • National Immigration Law Center — policy explainers, kept current as rules change.
Common status terms, in plain language
Refugee, asylee, green card, and more
+

Refugee — approved for resettlement before arriving in the US; eligible for resettlement benefits from day one.

Asylee — granted protection after arriving and applying from inside the US; similar benefits to refugees once approved.

Lawful permanent resident (green card holder) — can live and work permanently; some federal benefits have a waiting period.

Naturalized citizen — full citizenship after meeting residency and civics requirements; eligible for all federal benefits and voting.

Exact eligibility and timelines change with federal policy — confirm your own situation with USCIS or legal aid rather than relying on this summary alone.

What can go wrong — and why it matters to get help
Consequences of common mistakes
+

A late filing, an incomplete form, or an unreported change of address can delay a case for months or put a status at risk. Unlicensed "notarios" or paid non-lawyers who promise a guaranteed outcome are a common source of real harm in immigrant communities — a notary public in the US does not have the same legal authority as in Ethiopia and cannot represent you in an immigration matter.

If you've already made a filing mistake, the fix is usually still to talk to a licensed attorney or accredited nonprofit as soon as possible, not to wait.
